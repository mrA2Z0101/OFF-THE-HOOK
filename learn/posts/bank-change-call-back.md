The scam that costs businesses the most isn't ransomware. It's a simple email saying **"our bank details have changed."** The FBI calls it business email compromise, and it costs businesses billions of dollars a year.

It works because the email often comes from the vendor's **real** account (it was hacked), or from a look-alike address, at a time when you're expecting an invoice anyway.

```email
From: Karen Wu <karen.wu@supplyco.com>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: RE: October statement
Verdict: phishing

Hi,

Quick update before you process October: ==we've moved our banking to a new institution|The #1 sign of payment fraud==. ==Please update our ACH details to the attached form for this and all future payments.|They want every future payment, not just one==

==I'm traveling this week so email is best.|A reason you can't call them==

Thanks,
Karen
```

This one comes from the vendor's real address, so even the sender check passes. Only the call-back rule catches it.

## The call-back rule

> [!TIP] Every bank change gets a phone call
> Call the vendor on a number **you already had**: from your vendor records, an old invoice or their website. Never the number in the email asking for the change.

Make it a habit with two more steps:

- **Two people approve** any change to where money goes.
- **Wait for the call-back** even when it's "urgent". Real vendors understand.

```quiz
A long-time vendor emails from their usual address asking you to update their bank account. The email includes a phone number "for any questions". What do you do?
- [ ] Update it: it's their real email address
- [ ] Call the number in the email to confirm
- [x] Call them on the number already in your vendor records
Why: Real accounts get hacked, and the scammer controls any number in the email. A number you already had is the only check they can't intercept.
```
