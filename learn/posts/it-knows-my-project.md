A few years ago, a phishing email said "Dear Customer". Now it says your name, your job title, your manager's name and the project you posted about last week.

None of that is hard to find. Your **LinkedIn profile**, your company's website, project announcements, news articles and press releases are all public. An AI tool can read them and write a perfectly personal email in seconds.

```email
From: Mark Ellis <mark.ellis@harborview-gc.com>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: Re: Riverside Medical Center - revised details
Verdict: phishing

Hi Alex,

Following up on the ==Riverside Medical Center|Public: your company posted about this project on LinkedIn== job. As discussed with ==Dana|Your manager's name is on your company's website==, the revised connection details are in the folder below. Please review before Thursday's coordination meeting.

==Open revised details|The link goes to a fake Microsoft sign-in page==
```

## So how do you tell?

Details aren't proof. The usual questions still work:

- **Who really sent it?** Do you actually know `harborview-gc.com`? Have you emailed Mark before?
- **What does it want?** A sign-in to "see the files" is the red flag.
- **Is it a reply to something you sent?** "Re:" in the subject is free to type.

> [!TIP] Share less in public
> You don't need to hide your job, but think twice before posting who approves payments, which vendors you use, or when your manager is traveling.

```quiz
An email mentions your real project, your manager by name and a meeting you actually have this week. It asks you to sign in to see "revised drawings". What does that prove?
- [ ] It must be real: a scammer couldn't know all that
- [x] Nothing: those details can all be found online
- [ ] It's real if the logo looks right
Why: Personal details are easy to collect and AI makes them easy to use. Check the sender and what it's asking for.
```
