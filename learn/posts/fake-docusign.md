Contracts, change orders, lien waivers, new-vendor forms: a lot of paperwork gets signed online now. A fake "please review and sign" email fits right in.

The button usually leads to a page asking you to **sign in with your email account** before you can "view the document". Real signing services don't need your email password.

```email
From: DocuSign <dse_notification@docusign-esign.net>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: Completed: Please review and sign - Subcontract Agreement Rev 2
Verdict: phishing

Northside Builders sent you a document to review and sign.

==REVIEW DOCUMENT|Goes to docusign-esign.net, not docusign.net or docusign.com==

==Sign in with your Microsoft account to access this document.|Signing services don't need your email password==

Do not share this email. It contains a secure link to DocuSign.
```

## How to check

- **Check the sender's domain.** Real DocuSign emails come from `docusign.net` or `docusign.com`.
- **Use the security code instead of the button.** Real DocuSign emails include a security code. Go to `docusign.com` yourself, choose to access documents, and enter the code.
- **Were you expecting it?** If not, ask the sender (by phone or a fresh email you start yourself) whether they sent it.

```quiz
A "DocuSign" request for a change order asks you to sign in with your work email to see the document. What does that tell you?
- [ ] It's normal for secure documents
- [x] It's a red flag: DocuSign doesn't need your email password
- [ ] It's safe if the DocuSign logo looks right
Why: Logos are easy to copy. Asking for your email password to "view" a document is the giveaway.
```
