title: "QR codes: the link you can't hover"
date: 2026-10-20
summary: A QR code hides where it goes, and your phone skips your work computer's protections. That's why scammers love them.
tags: links, phone

With a normal link, you can hover and see where it goes. With a **QR code**, you can't, until you've already scanned it. And when you scan it, you're on your **phone**, which usually doesn't have your work computer's filters and protections.

That's why phishing emails increasingly look like this:

```email
From: HR Benefits <benefits@yourcompany-hr.com>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: Action required: 2027 benefits enrollment
Verdict: phishing
Attachment: Benefits_Enrollment.pdf

Open enrollment closes Friday. ==Scan the QR code in the attached PDF with your phone to confirm your elections.|A QR code moves you off your protected computer==

==Employees who don't confirm will lose coverage.|A threat to rush you==
```

The QR code in the PDF goes to a fake Microsoft sign-in page that's designed to look good **on a phone**, where the address bar is tiny and easy to ignore.

## How to stay safe

- **Don't scan QR codes in emails** to sign in to anything. Go to the site or app yourself.
- **Look at the address** your phone camera shows before tapping it. If it's not the real site, stop.
- **Watch for QR codes in public too**: stickers over parking meter codes and fake "pay here" signs are common.
