For years the advice was simple: look for typos, odd grammar and "Dear Valued Costumer". That advice is out of date. Anyone can now ask an AI tool to write a polite, perfectly spelled email in the style of your bank, your IT team or your boss, in seconds and in any language.

So a clean, professional email tells you **nothing** about whether it's safe.

## What still gives a scam away

AI can fix the writing. It can't change what the scammer needs from you. Almost every phishing email wants one of these:

- **Your sign-in**: "confirm your password", "your mailbox is full", "a document was shared with you"
- **Your money**: gift cards, a wire, an urgent invoice, "our bank details have changed"
- **Your information**: your cell number, your W-2, a code that was just texted to you
- **A click or a download** that leads to one of the above

> [!TIP] Grade the request, not the writing
> Ask yourself: *what does this email want me to do, and what happens if I'm wrong?* If the answer involves a password, money or personal details, check it another way first.

## Try it

This one reads perfectly. Click **Show red flags** to see what gives it away.

```email
From: Microsoft 365 Admin <admin@m365-mailcenter.com>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: Action required: storage limit reached
Verdict: phishing

Hello,

Your mailbox has reached ==98% of its storage limit|A classic reason to make you sign in==. To avoid interruption to incoming mail, please ==verify your account within 24 hours|Deadlines are there to stop you from thinking==.

==Review storage settings|The button goes to m365-mailcenter.com, not microsoft.com==

Thank you,
Microsoft 365 Team
```

Nothing is misspelled. But the sender isn't Microsoft, it wants you to sign in, and it's rushing you. That's the whole scam.

```quiz
A polished email from "Payroll" asks you to confirm your direct deposit details through a link. There are no spelling mistakes. What should you do?
- [ ] It's well written, so it's probably fine
- [x] Go to the payroll system yourself (or call HR) instead of using the link
- [ ] Reply and ask if it's real
Why: Good writing proves nothing now. Replying goes to the scammer. Going to the site yourself, or calling someone you know, is the only check that works.
```
