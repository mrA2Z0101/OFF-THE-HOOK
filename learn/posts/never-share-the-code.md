---
title: Never share the code you just got
date: 2026-10-20
summary: That six-digit code texted to you is the last lock on your account. Anyone who asks you for it is trying to open that lock.
tags: accounts, phone
---

Sign-in codes (the ones sent by text, email or an app) exist so that a stolen password **isn't enough** to get into your account. That's why scammers work so hard to get you to read the code to them.

It usually goes like this: they already have your password, they try to sign in, the site texts *you* a code, and then they contact you pretending to be IT, your bank or Microsoft:

```email
From: IT Security <it-security@yourcompany-support.com>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: Account verification required
Verdict: phishing

Hi,

We detected an unusual sign-in on your account. ==To confirm it's you, reply with the 6-digit code we just texted to your phone.|Nobody legitimate ever asks for your code==

==If we don't hear back in 15 minutes your account will be locked.|Pressure to stop you thinking==

IT Security Team
```

> [!REDFLAG] The rule with no exceptions
> Real IT, banks and Microsoft **never** ask you to read, forward or reply with a sign-in code. The code only ever gets typed into the sign-in page you opened yourself.

## If you got a code you didn't ask for

Someone may have your password. Don't share the code, **change your password**, and tell IT.

```quiz
Someone calls saying they're from your bank's fraud team. To "verify your identity", they ask for the code they just texted you. What do you do?
- [ ] Read it to them: they're protecting your account
- [x] Hang up and call the number on the back of your card
- [ ] Read only the first three digits
Why: The code lets whoever has it into your account. Real fraud teams don't ask for it. Calling the number you already have is the safe check.
```
