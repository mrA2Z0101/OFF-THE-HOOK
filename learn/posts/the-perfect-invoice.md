It used to be easy to spot a fake invoice: a blurry logo, wrong fonts, odd wording. Now a scammer can copy a real vendor's invoice, or have AI build one, and it will look **exactly right**.

So don't judge an invoice by how it looks. Judge it by these three things:

1. **Do you expect it?** Is there a real order, job or PO behind it?
2. **Has anything changed?** New bank account, new address to pay, new contact person.
3. **Is it rushing you?** "Past due", "final notice", "pay today to avoid a hold".

```email
From: Accounts Receivable <ar@steelsupply-billing.com>
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: Invoice 88231 - FINAL NOTICE
Verdict: phishing
Attachment: INV-88231.pdf

Hello,

Please find attached invoice 88231. ==Our records show this is past due and your account will be placed on hold Friday.|Pressure, often about an invoice you've never seen==

==Please note our new remittance details in the attached invoice.|A new bank account is the real goal==

Thank you for your prompt payment.
```

> [!WARNING] A perfect PDF proves nothing
> Logos, letterheads and signatures are easy to copy. The only thing that proves an invoice is real is checking it through a channel you already trust.

## What to do

- Match it against your **PO or job records**.
- If anything about payment changed, **call the vendor** on the number already in your system.
- Send unexpected invoices to whoever approves payments, with a note that you couldn't confirm them.

```quiz
A flawless invoice arrives from a vendor you use, but the bank details are different from last month. What's the right move?
- [ ] Pay it: it matches their usual invoice exactly
- [ ] Reply to the email to confirm the new account
- [x] Call the vendor on the number already on file before paying
Why: Copies can be perfect, and replies go to the scammer. A known phone number is the check they can't fake.
```
